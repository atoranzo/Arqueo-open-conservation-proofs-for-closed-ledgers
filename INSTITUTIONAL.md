# ZK-SSL — Could this be of interest to central banks and financial institutions?

**Honest assessment, not marketing material.**

This document answers the questions a technical team at a central bank, a
Tier-1 consortium, or a financial market infrastructure would ask. Each
answer separates **what exists and has been measured** from **what would
be required**.

It is written this way for a practical reason: in a conversation with such
counterparts, any unverifiable claim is detected on the first inspection of
the repository, and contaminates everything else. A document that states
its limits is the only kind that survives that scrutiny.

---

## Short answer

**Could it be of interest as deployable infrastructure?** No. There is no
network, no consensus, it has not been audited, and it has never processed
a real transaction.

**Could it be of interest as technical evidence?** Yes, on one specific
point: **it provides reproducible measurements of what cryptographic
privacy with provable compliance actually costs**, plus eight design
findings not documented elsewhere.

That distinction is the difference between a conversation that progresses
and one that ends.

---

## 1. Is it modular, decentralized, and post-quantum infrastructure?

Three adjectives, three different answers.

### Modular — **yes**

Eight independent circuits, a state layer separate from the circuits,
proof generation separate from proof application, and an ISO 20022 bridge
decoupled from the core. The architecture allows replacing the proof
system without rewriting the business logic: in fact, the same circuit is
implemented in five.

### Decentralized — **NO**

**It is a single node.** Whoever operates it:

- Sees every balance.
- Orders operations and can censor them.

Both require distributed consensus, which is **not implemented** and is a
distributed-systems problem rather than a cryptographic one. Estimated at
20–40 weeks of specialized work.

The only thing closed in that direction: a **chained transition log** that
prevents rewriting history in secret. Publishing 32 bytes commits to the
entire history. It is the Certificate Transparency construction — it does
not prevent misbehavior, it makes it detectable.

### Post-quantum — **yes, and this is the most solid property**

The cryptographic core depends **exclusively on hash functions**
(Rescue-Prime over the Goldilocks field, with FRI). There are no elliptic
curves or pairings on the critical path.

This matters for a financial infrastructure deployment horizon, where the
system must remain secure decades after going live.

**A technical caveat worth knowing**: the declared 127 bits of security are
**conjectured**. *Provable* security in that configuration lies between 29
and 63 bits. Closing the gap raises proof size from 36.7 KB to 125.6 KB.
That distinction is rarely made explicit, and is directly relevant to
parameter selection under regulatory criteria.

### Sovereign identity — **partially**

There are spend keys and cryptographically derived identities with domain
separation: a custodian key cannot masquerade as an account holder, or
vice versa.

**There are no DIDs, no verifiable credentials, and no SSI in the W3C
standard sense.** The identity component of the original design is
implemented at the level of cryptographic authority, not digital identity.

## 2. Does it resolve the tension between instant settlement and banking secrecy?

**It demonstrates that the tension is resolvable, and measures the cost. It
does not resolve it in production, because there is no production.**

### What has been demonstrated

An operation can be **private with respect to third parties** — who see
only a proof — and simultaneously **verifiable by a supervisor**, without
that supervisor having ledger access or any master key.

The mechanism is selective disclosure with three modes:

| Mode | The holder proves | The supervisor learns |
|---|---|---|
| Exact | My balance is X | The balance |
| Minimum | My balance exceeds X | Only that |
| **Band** | My balance lies between X and Y | Only the range |

Band mode satisfies a common supervisory requirement — confirming that a
position lies within a range — **without revealing the figure**.

### The structural property that makes it different

The proof is generated by **the holder**. The supervisor verifies it with a
free function.

**There is no custodial key to steal** in order to obtain general access to
balances. There is no supervisory backdoor.

The trade-off is stated: if the holder refuses to cooperate, the system
offers no forced-disclosure mechanism. Supervision is cooperative, not
coercive. For a regulated deployment, that is a policy decision requiring
evaluation.

### What is NOT resolved

**There is no cross-border settlement**, because there is no network layer.
The system runs on a single node; there is no inter-institutional
protocol, no cross-jurisdictional atomicity, and no correspondent banking
management.

**There is message-level interoperability**: the layer consumes `pacs.008`
and returns `pacs.002` with the proof attached and real ISO 20022 reason
codes (`AM04` insufficient funds, `AC01` incorrect account, `AG01`
transaction forbidden, and so on). A receiving system understands the
rejection without knowing this implementation.

That is a genuine interoperability component, but **it is not cross-border
settlement**.

## 3. What cryptographic technology forms the core?

**STARK/FRI over the Goldilocks field**, with the Rescue-Prime hash
function and AIR arithmetization.

### Why that choice, and not the fastest one

Five paradigms were evaluated by implementing the same circuit in each:

| | Groth16 | Halo2/IPA | **STARK/FRI** | PLONK/KZG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trusted ceremony | Per circuit | None | **None** | Universal |
| Setup | 438 ms | 16.3 s | **none** | 26.3 s + 12.8 s |
| Proving | 422 ms | 4.86 s | **39 ms** | 6.85 s |
| Verification | 5 ms | 91 ms | **1 ms** | 8 ms |
| Proof size | **192 B** | 4,096 B | 36.7 KB | 1,008 B |
| Post-quantum | No | No | **Yes** | No |

**Groth16 was rejected despite being faster and producing proofs 320×
smaller.**

The reason is the one that should matter most to a central bank: Groth16
and PLONK/KZG **require a trusted setup ceremony**. A set of participants
generates parameters from a secret that must then be destroyed. If they
collude and retain it, **they can forge proofs and create money without
leaving a detectable trace**. Forged proofs verify correctly; there is no
subsequent detection mechanism.

For an institution whose mandate includes monetary sovereignty, that
dependency is permanent — it does not expire — and unauditable — one
cannot verify that the secret was destroyed.

It is the only decision in the project taken **against** the performance
figures.

## 4. Where does the infrastructure physically operate?

**On a development machine. There is no deployment.**

No operational infrastructure exists: no data centers, no distributed
nodes, no production environment, no service-level agreements.

The system is a set of Rust libraries plus a state layer with local
persistence. It runs wherever it is compiled.

### What does exist operationally

| Capability | Status |
|---|---|
| Persistence with integrity verification at startup | ✅ |
| Atomic writes (one batch per operation) | ✅ |
| Exportable snapshots with verification | ✅ |
| Encryption at rest (XChaCha20-Poly1305) | ✅ |
| Live replication | ❌ |
| High availability | ❌ |
| Automated disaster recovery | ❌ |

Encryption at rest carries an operational consequence worth knowing: **the
key is supplied by the operator at startup**, so the node cannot restart
unattended. Storing it alongside the data would protect nothing.

## 5. Where are the zero-knowledge proofs processed?

**On the client**, and that is a relevant design decision.

```
1. The client requests the public view of its account
2. It computes the nullifier LOCALLY, using its key
3. It requests materials: Merkle paths and state data
4. It generates the proof ON ITS OWN MACHINE
5. It submits the settlement; the layer verifies and applies
```

**The spend key never reaches the node operator.** An attacker who
intercepts the materials — Merkle paths, balances, nonces — cannot
generate the proof without the key.

### Computational cost

| Operation | Prove | Verify |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer | ~620 ms | ~4 ms |
| Issuance | ~105 ms | ~2 ms |
| Audit | ~250 ms | ~1.5 ms |

**Verification costs 0.5–0.8% of proving.** That asymmetry is the economic
property making the model viable: cost falls on the party producing the
proof, not on the party accepting it.

### The limitation

Generating a proof requires ~620 ms and significant memory. A lightweight
client — a point-of-sale terminal, a mobile device — cannot comfortably do
this.

Delegating it to a third party **without handing over the key** requires
verifying a signature in-circuit. This is technically viable with
hash-based signatures (Winternitz, consistent with the post-quantum
design) but **is not implemented**: estimated at roughly 8,000 additional
trace rows.

## 6. Where are global state and accounts stored?

**In a local embedded database** (`sled`), on the same machine running the
node.

### State structure

| Structure | Depth | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Account tree | 32 (4.29 B positions) | `H(H(identity, balance), nonce)` |
| Nullifier tree | 32 | Spend markers |
| Frozen tree | 24 (16.7 M) | Blocked accounts |

The trees are **sparse**: only occupied leaves are materialized, and empty
subtrees are represented by their canonical root. Memory is proportional to
actual usage, not to the address space.

Additionally there are public scalars: total supply, regulatory limit,
issuance cap, custodian and governance set roots, and three intervention
counters.

### Storage guarantees

**Integrity verification at startup.** The trees are reconstructed from
stored leaves, their roots recomputed, and compared against those of the
last shutdown. If they do not match, **startup fails**.

Without that check, the node would generate perfectly valid proofs of
transitions over a ledger that is not the real one — cryptographically
undetectable from outside.

**Atomic writes.** Each operation writes accounts, nullifiers, metadata,
and log entries in a single batch. If the process dies mid-way, the ledger
remains consistent: the operation is lost, integrity is not.

**Encryption at rest.** Values are encrypted with authenticated
XChaCha20-Poly1305. It protects against theft of the disk or of a backup;
**not against the operator**, who sees balances in memory.

### The scaling limit, quantified

**One thousand transfers accumulate 59.1 MB of proofs.** That is the
system's real limit, and it is measured rather than estimated.

Resolving it requires recursive aggregation or batched proofs, neither of
which is implemented.

## 7. How is transaction privacy guaranteed?

### What a third party cannot see

Neither identities, nor balances, nor amounts. Only a cryptographic proof
and the public state roots, which are commitments and reveal nothing about
their contents.

**Only the holder can compute their nullifier**, since it derives from
their spend key. This prevents an observer from precomputing the
nullifiers of other accounts to monitor when they spend.

### A finding on proof size

We tested whether proof size leaks information about the amount. **It is
not constant**: it varies by 5.4% between operations.

The measured correlation between amount and size is **+0.008 on a
logarithmic scale** — essentially null — which rules out a gross leak.

⚠️ With 16 samples that is **weak evidence, not proof**. Ruling out the
leak would require hundreds of samples and statistical analysis, which has
not been performed. It is documented as an open question.

### ⚠️ What the operator DOES see

**Every balance.** The layer maintains the state, therefore it knows it.

This system's privacy is **against third parties who see only proofs**,
not against whoever maintains the ledger.

For an institution, this means the current model applies to a scenario
where **the entity operating the node legitimately has access to the data**
— a central bank over its own records, for instance — but **not** to a
multi-institutional scenario where participants must not see one another.

That second scenario requires consensus, and with it, state replication
across mutually distrusting parties.

## 8. How is quantum resistance ensured?

**By construction**: the core depends exclusively on hash functions.

### What it uses and what it does not

| Component | Primitive | Quantum-vulnerable? |
|---|---|---|
| State commitment | Rescue-Prime (hash) | No |
| Proof system | FRI (hash + Reed-Solomon) | No |
| Identities and nullifiers | Rescue-Prime | No |
| Encryption at rest | XChaCha20-Poly1305 | Effective security halved (Grover); acceptable |
| **Elliptic curves** | **None on the critical path** | — |

Shor's algorithm breaks the discrete logarithm and factoring problems on
which elliptic curves and pairings rely. **Groth16, PLONK/KZG, Halo2, and
Nova use them; STARK/FRI does not.**

Grover halves the effective security of hash functions, which is accounted
for in parameter selection.

### The appropriate caveat

The quantum resistance of the proof system is a property of the
construction, not a promise. But the caveat from §1 bears repeating: **the
declared 127 bits are conjectured**; provable security in that
configuration lies between 29 and 63 bits.

For a deployment under regulatory criteria, that distinction should be
resolved explicitly during parameter selection, and the cost is measured:
36.7 KB versus 125.6 KB per proof.

## 9. So what could actually be of interest?

With all of the above on the table, there are three honest answers.

### As a data source for a paradigm decision

Any institution evaluating zero-knowledge proofs for settlement must
choose a proof system. This work provides **reproducible measurements of
the same circuit across five paradigms**, with findings absent from the
literature:

- AIR arithmetization **lacks copy constraints**, which opens a silent
  soundness gap when implementing state updates. This is not discovered by
  measuring SHA-256.
- The Goldilocks field is **too narrow for identities**: 64 bits mean
  collision in 2³².
- The **gap between conjectured and provable security**.
- **PLONK/KZG proved the slowest prover** of the four curve-based systems.

### As a quantified answer to "what does privacy cost?"

The debate on privacy in central bank digital currencies is rich in
positions and poor in figures. Here there are some: **verification costs
4 ms, proving 620 ms, and one thousand transfers are 59 MB**.

And there is an ECB data point that frames the problem: in its public
consultation, privacy was the most valued aspect (43%), and **fewer than
one in ten individual responses supported full anonymity**. Privacy
without total anonymity is precisely what selective disclosure delivers.

### As a demonstration that the problem is tractable

That these properties are simultaneously constructible — privacy, provable
compliance, money conservation, selective audit, no trusted ceremony — was
not evident before building it. It is now measured and reproducible.

## 10. What would an institutional pilot require?

In order of dependency:

| Requirement | Status | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| **External audit** | Not performed | Prerequisite to everything else |
| **Distributed consensus** | Not implemented | 20–40 weeks |
| Proof delegation to third parties | Not implemented | 4–8 weeks |
| Proof aggregation | Not implemented | 6–12 weeks |
| Replication and high availability | Not implemented | 3–6 weeks |
| Operational key rotation | Partial | 2–4 weeks |
| Freeze expiry and recorded justification | Not implemented | 2–4 weeks |

**External audit is the only one that is a precondition rather than a
capability.** No amount of self-testing substitutes for it, and without it
the rest should not be deployed.
